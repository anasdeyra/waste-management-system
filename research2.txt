Algorithmic Optimization of Single-Vehicle Collection Tours: A Literature Review on Capacity-Constrained Routing, Local Search Heuristics, and Waste Management Evaluation1. Research Question and Problem FramingThe primary research question driving this evaluation asks to what extent intelligent collection-tour optimization can reduce total travel distance and estimated collection duration compared with a documented baseline, while satisfying identical vehicle-capacity and collection-service constraints.To analyze this question, the theoretical relationships between classical combinatorial optimization formulations and the specific single-vehicle, capacity-constrained waste collection model analyzed in this project must be established.The Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) represents the foundational model of tour optimization. Given a set of $N$ collection points and a distance matrix $C = (c_{ij})$ representing pairwise travel costs, the symmetric TSP seeks a permutation of nodes that minimizes the total cost of a closed Hamiltonian cycle visiting every node exactly once and returning to the depot. The TSP is proven to be $NP$-hard; computational complexity scales exponentially with node count $N$, rendering exact solutions computationally expensive for real-time applications as $N$ grows.The Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) extends the TSP by incorporating physical operational constraints. In a standard CVRP, a fleet of $m$ vehicles stationed at a central depot services $N$ nodes, where each node $i$ has a demand $q_i$ and each vehicle has a maximum payload capacity $Q$. The objective is to design a set of minimum-cost routes such that every node is visited once, total demand on any route does not exceed $Q$, and all routes start and terminate at the depot.The project under study focuses on a single-vehicle, capacity-constrained tour planning module. This model bridges the single-vehicle structure of the TSP and the capacity restrictions of the CVRP. Rather than solving a simultaneous multi-vehicle fleet partitioning problem, the software module plans one single-vehicle tour at a time. However, unlike a basic TSP—where a vehicle visits all $N$ stops in a single loop regardless of weight—the vehicle capacity constraint $Q$ imposes intermediate return trips to the central depot whenever accumulated volume $\sum q_i$ reaches $Q$.The evaluation metrics combine total travel distance $D$ and total estimated duration $T$. Estimated tour duration is modeled as the sum of travel time and fixed service times spent loading waste at each stop:$$T = \sum_{i=0}^{N} \sum_{j=0}^{N} x_{ij} \cdot \frac{d_{ij}}{v} + \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i$$where $x_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether edge $(i, j)$ is traversed, $d_{ij}$ is the Euclidean distance, $v$ is the average vehicle travel speed, and $s_i$ is the service time at collection point $i$. Because service time $s_i$ is additive and invariant across route permutations for a given set of stops, reducing total Euclidean distance $D$ leads to a direct linear reduction in travel duration. However, because service time acts as a fixed additive baseline ($N \cdot s_{\text{avg}}$), percentage improvements in total duration $T$ will naturally appear smaller than percentage reductions in travel distance $D$.2. Thematic SynthesisExact Methods versus Heuristic ApproachesThe operations research literature divides vehicle routing methodologies into exact algorithms and approximate heuristics. Exact approaches—including Branch-and-Bound, Branch-and-Cut, and Dynamic Programming—guarantee globally optimal solutions by systematically exploring the search space and pruning non-promising sub-trees. As documented by Laporte (1992), exact algorithms for the CVRP can consistently solve instances up to approximately 50 to 100 nodes within reasonable time limits, depending on constraint tightness. However, because the underlying mathematical formulations are $NP$-hard, the worst-case computational complexity grows exponentially with node count $N$, scaling at $O(2^N)$ or factorial state space $(N-1)!$.In software engineering applications where rapid route generation is required, exponential runtime growth is problematic. Heuristic procedures address this trade-off by sacrificing guaranteed global optimality in exchange for polynomial runtimes. Approximate methods are split into constructive heuristics, which build an initial feasible tour from scratch, and local search heuristics, which iteratively improve an existing tour. The project under review evaluates this progression by comparing a non-optimized baseline (input selection order) against a capacity-aware constructive heuristic (Nearest Neighbor) and a local-search-enhanced pipeline (Nearest Neighbor combined with 2-opt).Nearest-Neighbor Construction and Structural VulnerabilitiesThe Nearest Neighbor (NN) algorithm represents one of the simplest constructive heuristics for spatial routing. Starting from an initial node (such as the central depot), the algorithm greedily selects the unvisited collection point closest to the current location, appends it to the tour, and repeats until all points are visited before returning to the depot.The primary advantage of Nearest Neighbor lies in its low computational complexity ($O(N^2)$) and straightforward implementation. However, theoretical analysis by Rosenkrantz, Stearns, and Lewis (1977) proves that Nearest Neighbor exhibits severe structural weaknesses. Because the algorithm makes localized greedy choices without evaluating global tour geometry, early choices consume conveniently located neighboring nodes. Consequently, near the end of the construction phase, the vehicle is often forced to make long, inefficient traverses across the bounding box to collect isolated nodes, producing self-intersecting edges. Rosenkrantz et al. demonstrated that for metric TSP instances with $N$ nodes, Nearest Neighbor can yield an approximation ratio of $\frac{1}{2}\lceil\log_2 N\rceil + \frac{1}{2}$ relative to the optimal tour, confirming that constructive greedy approaches alone are insufficient for robust route optimization.2-Opt Local Search and Geometric UncrossingTo correct the structural errors inherent in constructive heuristics, local search techniques modify existing tours. The 2-opt algorithm, introduced by Croes (1958) and expanded by Lin and Kernighan (1973), is the foundational local search procedure for symmetric routing problems.The mechanics of a 2-opt move involve selecting two non-adjacent edges, $(v_i, v_{i+1})$ and $(v_j, v_{j+1})$, removing them from the tour, reversing the internal path sequence between $v_{i+1}$ and $v_j$, and reconnecting the segments via edges $(v_i, v_j)$ and $(v_{i+1}, v_{j+1})$. The move is executed if and only if the change yields a net reduction in travel distance:$$\Delta D = (d_{v_i, v_j} + d_{v_{i+1}, v_{j+1}}) - (d_{v_i, v_{i+1}} + d_{v_j, v_{j+1}}) < 0$$In two-dimensional Euclidean space, intersecting tour segments violate the triangle inequality ($d_{ac} + d_{cb} > d_{ab}$). As Croes established, 2-opt uncrosses these intersecting edges, systematically restoring geometric efficiency. The local search continues iteratively until no further 2-edge swap yields $\Delta D < 0$, at which point the tour is certified as 2-optimal. Lin and Kernighan (1973) demonstrated that higher-order exchanges ($k$-opt where $k \ge 3$) can achieve further distance reductions, but at the cost of increased algorithmic complexity. For lightweight academic software systems, pairing Nearest Neighbor with 2-opt offers an effective balance between execution speed and distance reduction.Vehicle Capacity and Collection Service ConstraintsIn waste management applications, physical operational constraints alter tour construction. While a standard TSP assumes infinite vehicle capacity, real-world collection vehicles possess a finite volume or weight threshold $Q$.When integrating capacity constraints into a single-vehicle tour model, accumulated payload $\sum q_i$ must be tracked along the generated sequence. When adding node $i+1$ would exceed $Q$, the algorithm inserts a depot visit trip: the vehicle departs node $i$, travels to the central depot to unload, and returns to node $i+1$. As established by Clarke and Wright (1964), capacity enforcement splits a single continuous spatial loop into discrete collection legs.Service times ($s_i$) introduce an additional operational baseline. In waste collection, service time includes the physical duration required to align the vehicle, operate hydraulic loading equipment, and empty containers. Incorporating service times into duration calculations shifts the relative impact of route optimization:$$\text{Travel Duration Savings (\%)} = \frac{T_{\text{baseline}} - T_{\text{optimized}}}{T_{\text{baseline}}} = \frac{\frac{D_{\text{base}} - D_{\text{opt}}}{v}}{\frac{D_{\text{base}}}{v} + \sum s_i}$$Because $\sum s_i$ remains constant regardless of the visit sequence, the percentage improvement in total duration $T$ will always be strictly lower than the percentage improvement in travel distance $D$. Literature in urban logistics emphasizes that evaluations must report both spatial distance metrics and operational duration metrics to prevent misleading performance claims.Applications and Evaluation Practices in Waste ManagementApplied literature in municipal solid waste routing highlights key differences between theoretical routing models and field deployments. Benchmark repositories such as TSPLIB, established by Reinelt (1991), evaluate routing algorithms using standardized distance matrices under idealized Euclidean metrics. These benchmarks provide reproducible environments to measure pure algorithmic performance.In contrast, municipal waste management studies must account for street network topologies, turn restrictions, traffic patterns, and vehicle capacities. Literature demonstrates that introducing heuristic route optimization to municipal waste collection yields substantial reductions in total travel distance under operational conditions. For software engineering prototypes that do not integrate Geographic Information Systems (GIS) or live GPS telemetry, synthetic Euclidean benchmarking remains the accepted standard. By generating spatial instances with controlled random seeds across varying node scales ($N \in \{10, 25, 50, 100\}$), developers can systematically measure algorithmic scalability, distance reductions, and duration savings without introducing external network noise.Performance Trade-Offs, Computational Complexity, and ReproducibilityEvaluating routing heuristics requires analyzing the trade-offs between solution quality, computational effort, and experimental reproducibility. Research Gap and Project PositioningEstablished Insights in Routing LiteratureThe operations research literature establishes several key principles regarding vehicle routing heuristics:Constructive greedy heuristics (e.g., Nearest Neighbor) quickly improve upon unmanaged or arbitrary visit sequences, but introduce structural errors near tour completion. Local search routines—specifically 2-opt edge inversions—systematically resolve spatial edge crossings in Euclidean space, yielding significant distance reductions over purely constructive methods. Integrating vehicle capacity constraints ($Q$) breaks continuous single-loop tours into multi-leg paths punctuated by depot unloading visits, altering tour geometry. Service times ($s_i$) introduce an additive lower bound to tour duration, meaning percentage duration reductions will be smaller than percentage travel distance reductions.Persistent Operational and Algorithmic ChallengesDespite extensive literature on vehicle routing, real-world deployment presents ongoing operational challenges. Real-world road networks violate Euclidean space due to one-way streets, turn restrictions, and varying speed limits, resulting in asymmetric distance matrices ($c_{ij} \neq c_{ji}$). Dynamic environment variables, such as traffic congestion and variable container fill rates, introduce real-time variations that static offline algorithms cannot anticipate. Furthermore, fully optimizing multi-vehicle fleets under time-window constraints requires solving complex CVRP formulations that exceed the capabilities of single-vehicle local search models.Methodological Rationale and Positioning of the Academic PrototypeThe academic prototype under study is positioned as a focused algorithmic evaluation tool for single-vehicle collection tours. The project adapts established concepts from classical literature: capacity-aware constructive logic adapted from classical CVRP principles (Clarke & Wright, 1964) to reset tour legs to the central depot when accumulated load reaches vehicle capacity $Q$; local search optimization adapting 2-opt edge inversion (Croes, 1958) to uncross self-intersecting segments in constructed routes; and controlled evaluation protocols adopting benchmark principles (Reinelt, 1991) by testing algorithms across identical, seeded synthetic scenarios with node counts of $N \in \{10, 25, 50, 100\}$.Comparing Input Order, Capacity-Aware Nearest Neighbor, and Nearest Neighbor plus 2-opt under controlled synthetic scenarios provides a sound experimental design. By holding spatial topologies, node demands, depot placement, and vehicle capacity constant, this protocol isolates the specific performance impact of greedy construction and local search uncrossing.Delineation of Supportable versus Non-Supportable ConclusionsTo maintain academic rigor, the evaluation framework must clearly delineate valid experimental conclusions from overextended claims, as structured below:DimensionSupportable Experimental ConclusionsNon-Supportable Overextended ClaimsOptimality ClaimsQuantifiable distance ($D$) and duration ($T$) percentage reductions over the input-order baseline.Claims of global tour optimality (2-opt reaches local optima only).Algorithmic ComparisonsMeasured incremental distance improvement of 2-opt local search over constructive Nearest Neighbor.Claims of state-of-the-art performance against advanced metaheuristics (LNS, GA).Duration DynamicsMathematical validation of the linear masking effect of service times on duration savings percentages.Claims of real-world municipal fleet savings under live traffic or GIS conditions.Scalability & BoundsEmpirical CPU execution time scaling across problem scales $N \in \{10, 25, 50, 100\}$.Claims of multi-vehicle fleet balancing or simultaneous route assignment.5.
